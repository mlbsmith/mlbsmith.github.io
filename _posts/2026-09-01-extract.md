Why wouldn't employees hit that easy button?

There are 2 problems I see looming:

- employees are 10x or 100x-ing their production and employers are the ones recouping that value (salaries ain't going up that much)
- the effort of this productivity boost take a mental and physical toll

So why wouldn't employees check out after an hour of work?
They are delivering what they are contractually obligated to.

It seems obvious to me that there is a reckoning coming.
For the sake of argument, put aside your feelings about the ethics of artificial intelligence, and large language models in general.
Consider a reality where all knowledge workers have access to an "easy" button that does boring, mundane work they do not want to.
Put aside, for a moment, what the quality of that work is.
The point is that the work is done, not its quality.
Most likely, you are working a boring job that you do not fundamentally care about, but you have agreed to do because it's a pretty good deal to exchange your time and mental effort for money.
A better deal than tilling soil and stressing about the weather, at least.

And, if you are working a boring job that you do not fundamentally care about, why _wouldn't_ you smash the easy button if it means you get a few more minutes to watch YouTube videos or gossip with your coworkers?
Obviously that is the natural response.
I can't believe anyone would assume otherwise.

Now, on the other side of the equation, there are those select few who care deeply about their work.
Maybe not the mission statement of their employer, but they take pride in their work and probably gain some measure of self-worth from being damn good at what they do.
These people are different.
These people will see that easy button, and they might be a bit skeptical.
They will say - and rightly so, I suspect - that "that button can't do the work as well as I can".
And they will resist, for a while.
But then, they'll grow curious.
They'll think about all the times they gave menial tasks to their less capable colleagues, and they will think "well, it can't be so bad as that".
Right again, I would say.
And they will poke and prod at that easy button.
They will understand its inner workings like they understand the flow of data from customers to the engineering team.
The way they understand how the most important product in the company actually works and how to sell that to propsective customers.
And they will realize, one day, that they have donned a mech-suit.

They are giants amongst mortals.

And they will poke and prod more and more and more, until they are doing as much work as the rest of their team combined, then the department, then, maybe, the company itself.
And they will sit back one day, running on 4 hours of sleep and adrenaline and pure joy at the _productivity_, and they will ask: "is this good?"

Is it good?
Who gets the benefits of the productivity boost from AI?
Is it the employees?
Up to a point, one could make the argument that the answer is yes.
But only up to a point.
And that point is hit pretty quickly by the average employee.
The one who does not care for the corporation to hit its quarterly earnings expectations.
They accrue the value from AI usage because they are simply doing less.
And filling their time with whatever they want.
They, quite quickly, I think, hit an equilibrium of using AI to do their job.
Possibly worse, but possibly slightly better if they keep the reigns tight.
They may get replaced when the next frontier models drop in 2027, and I'm sure that anxiety is making it hard to relax.
But the work?
The work is easy, right now.

Now, consider the high performers.
The ones feverishly learning, building, striving to do more, more, more.
Who gets the benefits of all that frenetic activity?
I'll give you a hint: it's not them.
It is the company.
If revenue goes up 30%, and AI spend cuts into 10% of that, you're looking at a 20% profit increase.
That ain't going to Alice, is it?

So the question becomes: what does Alice do?
We know what Bob does.
Bob stresses about his job, but mostly does nothing, and enjoys life.
Because he doesn't care, and he is not responsible for the 20% margin increase.
Alice is though, and if she's smart, which I think she is, she knows it.
So what does she do?
A high achiever on the edge of burnout with rock-solid intuition that she is getting milked for every last joule in her brain.
And a salary increase of, what, 10%?
The company makes 10 million, Alice gets 10 thousand.
Seem fair?
I don't think so.

What's a girl to do then?
Leave?
Fight back.
Become Bob?
I don't know the answer to that one, but I think it's a question every single company should be asking themselves.
